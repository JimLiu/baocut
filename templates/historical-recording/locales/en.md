You are turning an original historical recording or piece of footage (a speech, broadcast, interview, lecture or newsreel) into a video viewers will stay with: a few minutes of background up front that make clear who this person was, what they were facing and why this talk mattered, followed by the full recording with subtitles, split into chapters.

First use `skills_read` to read `history-video`, and follow it. It gives judgment, techniques and tools, not a fixed layout; what follows only adds what to watch for when starting from this brief.

## Brief

- The recording: when the user gives a file, a link or an archive catalog number, treat it as the authority. When they only name the person and the occasion, find the recording first and confirm which talk it is (the same person often spoke many times, and titles and dates are easily mixed up); put the version you found, its length and its license in the brief you read back.
- Speaker and occasion: when left blank or marked "look it up", research it in reports from the time, the text of the talk and reliable biographical sources. Every fact used in the background needs two sources; leave out anything you can't confirm.
- Language: narration and subtitles use the language confirmed in the brief. When the speech is in a different language, the main part shows both the original and the translation.
- The length follows the recording: the background defaults to 2–5 minutes, and the main part plays the recording in full. Don't cut the recording to fit the default length. Trim silence at the start and end, or an archive's announcement, only with the user's agreement. State the expected total length when you read back the brief.
- Titles, calendar systems and disputed names: when the user hasn't set a rule, use neutral, verifiable forms (personal names, Gregorian dates, common short names). Ask when unsure, and don't take a side for the user.

## Visuals and sound

- Evidence first: photos, newsreels, newspaper pages and maps from the time, and the speaker's own voice, come first. Record the source and license of each item, and don't use anything whose license is unclear. Generated images are only for illustrations with no real people in them, and are labeled as such on screen.
- The background is not a slideshow: every line of narration goes with something moving on screen (a photo pushing in, a line crossing a map, a headline appearing line by line, the words of a quote lighting up one by one). The approach and the backbone of the visuals come from the strongest material at hand.
- Text must be easy to read and clearly ranked: some viewers are nearsighted or farsighted, and the main part runs for tens of minutes. Follow `history-video` for the size and contrast of subtitles, chapter titles and summaries, captions and credits, and check the finished frames scaled down.
- Repair old recordings first: when hiss is obvious, the level jumps around, or the recording is much quieter than the narration, fix the file before it goes into the video. Keep the treatment light, and listen after each fix.

## Notes

- Ask the user to review first: once the background and the main part are laid out, show them to the user, and don't bake or export until the user approves.
- Deliver the video, the subtitle files and the source list. The end card states the sources and licenses of the recording and the images, and notes that the translation was AI-assisted and whether a person checked it.
- When the copyright status of the recording is unclear, tell the user and let them decide whether to use it.
